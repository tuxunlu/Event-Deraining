Event-camera deraining

How EvORSP-3T removes rain

The whole network is one idea applied twice: sort the signal into bins, turn down the bins where rain lives, add everything back up. What follows builds that up one piece at a time.

The problem

You cannot tell rain from scene by looking at one moment

An event camera reports one thing: this pixel just got brighter or darker, at this instant. A raindrop crossing a pixel and a building edge crossing the same pixel produce the same report. Nothing about a single event says which it was.

What this means The information that separates rain from scene is not in appearance. It is in how the signal behaves over space and time. So the network's first job is to convert behaviour into something it can measure.

The core idea

Treat it like a graphic equaliser

An audio equaliser splits sound into frequency bands — bass, mid, treble — and lets you turn each one down independently. The bands add back to the original, so if you move nothing, nothing changes.

EvORSP does exactly this to an event stream. It splits the signal into bins by how fast a pixel is changing and which direction the structure points, turns down the bins that look like rain, and adds the rest back up.

What the locked band does and does not buy Anything that stays put across time is, by definition, part of the scene, and rain never stays put — so leaving that band ungated is the right instinct. We then claimed the strong version of it: that the network therefore cannot delete the static world, however it is trained. We tested that claim directly and it is false. The band really does leave the equaliser untouched, but every one of the network's other twenty-eight thousand weights sits downstream of it and is free to undo the protection. Measured, they undo it almost completely. The full account, and a version of the network where the guarantee does hold, are in the last section.

Split one — by speed

Sorting by how fast a pixel flickers

Take each pixel's history over the window and ask how quickly it is changing. A wall changes not at all. A tree in wind changes slowly. A raindrop produces one sharp flicker and vanishes. Those land in different bins.

This entire stage costs six numbers — three bin centres and three bin widths. That is the whole temporal machinery.

Split two — by angle

Sorting by which way structure points

Rain falls in lines. A streak has a direction, and drops in one storm mostly share it. Scene structure points every which way. So a second sort, by orientation, concentrates rain into a few bins while scene spreads across all of them.

The decision

The sliders are set per pixel, not per frame

This is the part that does the actual work, and the only part that is really "learned". A small network looks at each neighbourhood and decides, for that location, how far to turn each slider down.

A measured choice This gate is ordinary convolutions. We tested a state-space scan in the same slot: it was worse (error 0.3641 against 0.3304), 3× larger, and 9× slower. Losing on all three at once is unusual, and it is why there is no Mamba-style component anywhere in this model.

The parts that encode the physics are almost free. Three quarters of every block is a conventional feed-forward layer doing nothing rain-specific.

That reframes a result that puzzled us for months: five different architectures, from 28,000 to 265,000 weights, all landed within 0.007 of each other. If the structured part is a rounding error in the parameter count, there was never much architecture there to vary. What moved the numbers was what we fed the network and what we asked it to predict — fixing the training target alone was worth 0.212, thirty times the entire spread across architectures.

A claim we had to withdraw

The locked band does not protect the scene end to end

For most of this campaign the page you are reading said something stronger than the truth: that because the persistent band has no slider, the network cannot delete the static world. It reads like an architectural guarantee. It is not one, and the way to find out was to attack it.

The test is direct. Feed the network a completely static input — a pattern that repeats identically in every time slice, so every pixel sits at the far left of the speed axis and lands entirely in the locked band. Then train the network to delete it anyway, and see how far it gets.

what was attackedsuppression achievedreading
the band decomposition alone1.0×immovable, exactly as claimed
the full shipped network530×protection undone
same network, locked band deleted363×the band was not what was helping
a plain CNN, no structure at all1332×reference point

The first row is the good news and the honest part of the original claim: attack the decomposition on its own and it does not move at all. Its six parameters have no way to drop the static band, and the optimiser cannot find one.

The third row is what settles the rest. If the locked band were carrying the guarantee, deleting it should have made the network dramatically easier to attack. It made it harder, which is to say the difference is noise and the band was contributing nothing to the outcome. The band is genuinely ungated where it is defined — and then every other weight in the network sits downstream of it, free to look at it and decide otherwise.

The exact multiplier for a full network wanders between roughly 400× and 700× across seeds, since it depends on how far one short adversarial run happens to get. The ordering, and the conclusion, do not move.

Why a bypass alone can never work The obvious repair is to wire the locked band straight to the output as z + res. That buys nothing. z is unbounded, so the learnable branch can simply emit a large negative number and cancel the bypass exactly. Any additive path can be cancelled by an unconstrained partner. To get a guarantee you have to bound the partner.

What the guarantee costs

It works, and it is not free. We trained it at three strengths on synthetic KITTI and two on the real rain rig, everything else held fixed:

A weak floor costs about 0.007 on both datasets. A floor strong enough to be worth quoting — keep-probability above 0.88 on any persistent pixel — costs 0.036 on KITTI and 0.046 on the rig.

We expected the real rig to be kinder, since real scenes contain far more genuine persistent structure for the guarantee to protect. It was harsher, and the damage is not spread evenly: on the two rain levels the unguarded model already found hardest, the strong guard gives up a further 0.10 each, against a median of 0.037 across the rest. The likely mechanism is the one that made this the interesting test in the first place — the rig generates rain from fixed nozzles, so some of its rain sits at fixed pixels, looks exactly like scene structure to the one cue this guarantee is built on, and is forcibly retained by it.

Where this leaves the design The locked band is a good inductive bias and a real part of why the model works. It is not a safety property, and we should not have described it as one. A genuine safety property is available, it is three lines of algebra, and it costs between 0.007 and 0.046 event-DA depending on how strong you want it — which is a trade worth offering, and not a claim worth making for free.
